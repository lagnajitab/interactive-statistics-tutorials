01 / Understanding the problem

What is different about two-way ANOVA?

One-way ANOVA compares groups formed by one factor. Two-way ANOVA studies two factors at the same time. This lets us investigate each factor's average effect and whether the effect of one factor depends on the level of the other factor.

Two factors and four combinations

Suppose Factor A is a material type (A1 or A2) and Factor B is a curing temperature (B1 or B2). The response is material strength. We collect observations for all four combinations: A1B1, A1B2, A2B1, and A2B2.

Each combination is called a cell. A cell mean is the average response from one combination. The four cell means are the basis for the comparisons shown here.

Three questions to investigate

  1. Main effect of A: Is the average response for A2 different from A1, averaging equally over B1 and B2?
  2. Main effect of B: Is the average response for B2 different from B1, averaging equally over A1 and A2?
  3. A × B interaction: Does the change from B1 to B2 depend on whether A is at level A1 or A2?
Understanding a main effect and an interaction

If B increases the response by 10 units at A1 and also by 10 units at A2, the effect of B does not depend on A. There is no interaction in those cell means. If B increases the response by 10 units at A1 but decreases it by 5 units at A2, the effect of B depends on A, so an interaction is present.

On an interaction plot, parallel lines mean the B1-to-B2 change is the same at both A levels. Nonparallel lines mean the changes differ. Lines can be nonparallel without crossing.

03 / Working through the calculations

How the three F-tests are calculated

The four cell means are used to calculate the main effects of A and B and the A × B interaction. Each estimated effect is then compared with its uncertainty, which depends on the within-cell variation and the number of observations per cell.

Calculation using your current settings

Change the sliders or select a scene above. The estimated effects and their F-statistics below update automatically.

Step 1. Calculate the main effects

Write the cell means as m11, m12, m21, and m22, where the first index identifies A and the second identifies B.

Effect A = (m21 + m22)/2 − (m11 + m12)/2

Effect B = (m12 + m22)/2 − (m11 + m21)/2

For each main effect, the two levels of the other factor receive equal weight. This remains the definition used by this tutorial when sample sizes differ.

Step 2. Calculate the interaction

First find the change from B1 to B2 at A1. Then find the same change at A2. Subtract the first change from the second.

B effect at A1 = m12 − m11
B effect at A2 = m22 − m21

A × B = (m22 − m21) − (m12 − m11)

If the two changes are equal, the interaction is zero and the lines are parallel. The interaction need not be zero just because the average main effects are zero.

Worked example: an interaction without main effects

Use the following four cell means from the crossing-lines example.

B1B2
A14060
A26040
  1. Average for A1 = (40 + 60)/2 = 50. Average for A2 = (60 + 40)/2 = 50. Therefore Effect A = 0.
  2. Average for B1 = (40 + 60)/2 = 50. Average for B2 = (60 + 40)/2 = 50. Therefore Effect B = 0.
  3. At A1, changing B from B1 to B2 changes the mean by 60 − 40 = +20.
  4. At A2, the same change in B changes the mean by 40 − 60 = −20.
  5. The interaction is −20 − (+20) = −40. B acts differently at the two levels of A even though both average main effects are zero.

The interaction plot for these values has crossing lines. In the interactive tool, choose Crossing lines to display this type of pattern.

Step 3. Account for variation and sample size

The simulator uses a common within-cell sample SD for all four cells. Its square is the error mean square, MSE = SD². Each effect is tested using a contrast of the four cell means.

F = (estimated contrast)² / [MSE × Σ(cᵢ²/nᵢ)]

Here cᵢ is the coefficient of a cell mean in the contrast, and nᵢ is the number of observations in that cell. The coefficients are:

Contrastm11m12m21m22
A−½−½+½+½
B−½+½−½+½
A × B+1−1−1+1

The term Σ(cᵢ²/nᵢ) accounts for precision. Larger cell sizes make this quantity smaller and can increase F when the cell means and SD stay fixed.

Step 4. Compare the three tests

Each test has numerator degrees of freedom 1. The error degrees of freedom are N − 4, where N is the total number of observations across the four cells.

The p-value answers how unusual its F would be if the corresponding null hypothesis were true. The null hypotheses are that Effect A = 0, Effect B = 0, and A × B = 0, respectively.

At the selected significance level α, reject the corresponding null hypothesis if p < α.

04 / Interpreting results

Reading main effects and interactions together

A main effect describes a difference averaged over the levels of the other factor. An interaction describes how that difference changes between levels. Both should be examined before interpreting the experiment.

How to read the interaction plot

Parallel lines: The B1-to-B2 change is the same at both levels of A. There is no interaction in these cell means.

Nonparallel lines: The B effect depends on A. The lines may diverge, converge, or cross.

Crossing lines: The relative ordering of A1 and A2 reverses between B1 and B2. Average main effects may be zero even when the interaction is substantial.

What a significant test means

A significant main effect of A means that the equally averaged A2 and A1 means differ. A significant main effect of B means the equally averaged B2 and B1 means differ.

A significant A × B interaction indicates that the effect of B differs between A1 and A2. When an interaction is present, examine the changes in B at each level of A before relying on average main effects alone.

Important distinction when cell sizes differ

With equal cell sizes, the three F-tests in this tool agree with the standard balanced 2 × 2 two-way ANOVA tests. With unequal cell sizes, the tool uses equally weighted cell-mean contrasts and their standard errors. These are adjusted tests of the displayed contrasts, not sequential sums-of-squares tests. The distinction matters when interpreting main effects in the presence of an interaction.

Suggested comparisons using the existing controls
  1. Choose Only Factor A. Compare the heights of the two lines. They should be parallel with no average B effect.
  2. Choose Both main effects. Both line separation and slope can be nonzero while the two lines remain parallel.
  3. Choose Fan-shaped interaction. The nonparallel lines indicate that the B effect differs between A1 and A2.
  4. Choose Crossing lines. Check the two average main effects and compare them with the nonzero interaction.
  5. Choose More within-group variation. The means remain in the same pattern, but the larger SD affects the three F-tests.
  6. Choose Unequal sample sizes. Keep the means fixed and change one cell size. The effect estimates remain the same, but their standard errors and F-statistics change.
Conditions for F-test inference

Interpretation of p-values assumes independent observations, normally distributed errors, and a common population variance across the four cells. The observations here are generated for demonstration and are constructed to have the selected sample means and sample SD.

This is a deterministic educational simulation of a 2 × 2 fixed-effects factorial experiment with a shared sample SD. The F-tests are equal-weight, cell-mean-contrast tests with error degrees of freedom N − 4. They coincide with standard balanced two-way ANOVA tests when all four cell sizes are equal. For unequal cell sizes, these are adjusted tests of equally weighted marginal-mean contrasts, not sequential ANOVA sums of squares; interpret main effects alongside the interaction.
