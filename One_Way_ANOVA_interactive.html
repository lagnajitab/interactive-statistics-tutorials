01 / Understanding the problem

Why do we use one-way ANOVA?

Suppose three groups receive different treatments, and we measure the same numerical response in each group. Their sample means will probably not be identical. One-way ANOVA helps determine whether the observed differences are large compared with the variation among observations within the groups.

One factor, three groups

A factor identifies how the observations are grouped. Here the factor has three levels, called A, B, and C. For example, A, B, and C could represent three methods of preparing a material. The response is the measured material strength.

Each observation is one measurement. The sample mean is the average of the observations in a group. The three means are compared using a single ANOVA test.

Why compare variation?

If the three means differ by a small amount but the observations within each group vary widely, the differences may be difficult to distinguish from ordinary variation.

If the means are far apart and the measurements within each group are tightly clustered, the evidence of a difference among group means is stronger. ANOVA measures these two kinds of variation and compares them.

The question tested by one-way ANOVA
H₀: μA = μB = μC
H₁: At least one population mean differs.

Here H₀ is the null hypothesis, H₁ is the alternative hypothesis, and μA, μB, μC denote the population means. Rejecting H₀ does not identify which particular groups differ.

Between-group variation

Measures how far each group mean is from the overall mean. Group means that are farther apart generally produce larger between-group variation.

Within-group variation

Measures how far individual observations are from their own group mean. This variation remains even when the group means are equal.

The F-statistic

Compares between-group variation with within-group variation, after accounting for their degrees of freedom. A larger F provides more evidence against H₀.

03 / Working through the calculations

From group means to the F-statistic

The ANOVA calculation has two parts: quantify the separation of the group means, then quantify the variation of observations around their own group means. Dividing the corresponding mean squares gives F.

  1. Grand mean: The nine observations have an overall mean of 8.
  2. Between-group sum of squares: SS between = 3(5 − 8)² + 3(8 − 8)² + 3(11 − 8)² = 54.
  3. Within-group sum of squares: In each group, the squared deviations from its mean add to 2. Therefore SS within = 2 + 2 + 2 = 6.
  4. Mean squares: MS between = 54 / (3 − 1) = 27 and MS within = 6 / (9 − 3) = 1.
  5. F-statistic: F = 27 / 1 = 27. With numerator degrees of freedom 2 and denominator degrees of freedom 6, the p-value is 0.001.

Here the group means are sufficiently far apart, relative to their within-group variation, to produce a large F-statistic.

What are sums of squares?

A sum of squares (SS) adds squared deviations. Squaring prevents positive and negative deviations from cancelling. SS between measures separation among group means; SS within measures variation of observations around their own means.

SS between = Σ nⱼ(ȳⱼ − ȳ)²
SS within = Σ Σ(yⱼᵢ − ȳⱼ)²

Here nⱼ is the size of group j, ȳⱼ is its sample mean, and ȳ is the grand mean.

Why divide SS by degrees of freedom?

SS between measures differences among the k group means. These differences have k − 1 degrees of freedom because, once the grand mean is fixed, the last group mean cannot vary independently of the others. SS within measures how observations differ from their group means. After calculating k group means from N observations, N − k degrees of freedom remain.

Each sum of squares therefore represents a different number of freely varying deviations. Dividing SS by its corresponding degrees of freedom gives a mean square (MS), which is a variance estimate. Under the usual ANOVA assumptions, MS within estimates the common population variance. MS between estimates that same variance when the population group means are equal, but can be larger when the means differ.

df between = k − 1 = 2
df within = N − k = N − 3
MS = SS / df
F = MS between / MS within

There are k = 3 groups and N observations in total.

04 / Interpreting results

What can we conclude from ANOVA?

Statistical significance is a statement about evidence against the null hypothesis. It is not a measure of how large or important the differences are.

F, p-value, and α

When H₀ is true, a large F is less likely than a small F. The p-value is the probability, assuming H₀, of obtaining an F-statistic at least as large as the one observed.

If p < α, reject H₀. The same decision can be made by comparing the observed F with its critical F for the selected α and degrees of freedom. If p is not below α, do not reject H₀.

What ANOVA does not establish

Rejecting H₀ indicates that not all population means are equal. The overall test does not specify which pairs of means differ. Not rejecting H₀ does not prove that the population means are identical.

ANOVA also does not tell us whether an observed difference is practically important. The actual group means should be considered along with the test result.

Conditions for the usual F-test

The standard one-way ANOVA inference assumes independent observations, normally distributed errors within groups, and equal population variances. The simulated data in this tool are constructed for demonstration; the reported calculations describe those displayed values.
