Full factorial experiment

What is a two-level factorial experiment?

Factors at two levels

Suppose concrete strength is measured using two material compositions. Material composition is factor A. Its two chosen settings are called levels.

We write the lower level as −1 and the higher level as +1. The signs identify settings; they are not measured values.

A = −1   or   A = +1

When there is more than one factor, each run specifies a level for every factor.

Three factors

AMaterial composition−1 / +1
BCuring temperature−1 / +1
CCuring duration−1 / +1

For example, one run could use A = +1, B = −1 and C = +1. The full factorial experiment includes every possible combination.

Factors, levels and runs

What do “run” and “replicate” mean?

Reading the experiment

FactorOne quantity set at different levels, such as temperature.
LevelOne of the two settings: −1 or +1.
RunOne experiment performed at a particular combination of levels.
ReplicateAnother performance of the same set of runs.

Example with three factors

Consider A = +1, B = −1 and C = +1. Performing an experiment at these settings counts as one run.

A 23 full factorial experiment has eight different combinations. One replicate therefore requires 8 runs.

If every combination is performed twice, the experiment has 16 runs in total, but still only eight distinct combinations.

The number of runs per replicate refers to how many combinations are included each time the design is performed.

Main effects

What is a main effect?

Changing one factor

Suppose A represents material composition. We compare concrete strength at A = +1 with strength at A = −1, considering the levels of B and C in the design.

The difference attributable to changing A is called the main effect of A.

When B and C are also being studied, we compare A over the different combinations of B and C, rather than looking at only one run.

What the sign column tells us

A = +1
High A
A = −1
Low A

The A column tells us which experimental results enter a comparison with a plus sign and which enter with a minus sign.

[A]

Later, this comparison is written as a contrast, [A], using the observed results from the selected runs.

A main effect concerns one factor. Whether we can examine it separately depends on which runs were selected.

Two-factor and three-factor interactions

What is a two-factor interaction?

The effect of A may depend on B

Suppose a change from low A to high A produces an increase of 4 when B is low, but an increase of 12 when B is high.

Level of BResult at A = −1Result at A = +1Change
B = −11014+4
B = +11022+12

The effect of A is different at the two levels of B.

Interaction columns

This is called an AB two-factor interaction. In a sign table, AB is formed by multiplying the signs in columns A and B.

AB = A × B

Likewise, AC and BC describe the other two-factor interactions.

The ABC three-factor interaction involves all three factors. Its sign column is found by multiplying A, B and C.

ABC = A × B × C

Cost grows exponentially

Why can a full 2k design be impractical?

Experimental cost

Each run can use specimens, technician time, materials, and equipment. If we add more factors, the total number of distinct combinations quickly becomes difficult to manage.

For 10 factors, there are 1,024 unique combinations. With two repetitions per combination, that becomes 2,048 actual experimental runs.

If the full experiment exceeds the available budget, a planned fraction can be used to study selected effects with fewer runs.

The fractional factorial idea

Use only a planned fraction

2k−p

k counts the factors. p tells us how many times the number of runs is halved. Each independent design generator halves the number of combinations selected.

Fraction = 1 / 2p

Examples

24−18 runshalf of 16

Why planned selection matters

Why not simply pick any four runs?

A poor selection

Suppose all four selected runs have A = +1. Then we never observe the result at A = −1.

aabacabc

There is no low-A group to compare with the high-A group. We cannot estimate an A main effect from these four runs.

A structured selection

Choose the four runs where the product of signs is ABC = +1.

abcabc

Every factor is high in two runs and low in two runs. This balance makes meaningful low-versus-high comparisons possible.

The rule ABC = +1 gives two high and two low runs for each factor. The sign table shows which effects cannot be separated.

Before the first example

Why is there a cost to using fewer runs?

The benefit

A 23 full factorial experiment needs eight runs per replicate. A 23−1 fractional factorial experiment uses four.

8 runs → 4 runs

Fewer experiments require less time and fewer materials.

The limitation

Some comparisons become indistinguishable when we use only four runs.

For example, the signs of A and BC can become identical in all selected rows. Then [A] = [BC].

This is called confounding. The two effects cannot be separated from the selected results.

The number of runs is reduced, but the ability to examine individual effects is also reduced.

Contrasts of main effects

How do we write a contrast?

Read the A column

For the four selected runs a, b, c, abc, A has a +1 sign in runs a and abc. It has a −1 sign in b and c.

Form the contrast [A] by adding the measured results corresponding to +1 and subtracting those corresponding to −1.

[A] = a + abc − b − c

Which result has which sign?

+ a
+ abc
− b
− c

The same method is used to write [B], [C], [AB], [AC] and [BC]. Each effect has a column of +1 and −1 signs.

In these equations, a, b, c, abc stand for the results obtained from the corresponding runs.

Method of Checking for Confounding

Why does multiplying by ABC reveal confounding?

Start with the selected runs

In the 23−1 design, we select only the runs for which ABC = +1.

Multiplying any sign column by +1 leaves that column unchanged. Therefore, multiplying A by ABC must give the same signs as A in these four runs.

A × ABC = A × (+1) = A

Now multiply the factor letters and simplify. Because A is either −1 or +1, A² = +1.

Calculate the product

A × ABC = A²BC = BC

Both expressions describe the same sign column:

A = BC

Thus A and BC are confounded. Their contrast calculations are the same, so [A] = [BC].

This equality applies to the selected sign columns. It does not mean that the individual physical effects of A and BC have the same value.

Reason for the multiplication: ABC is +1 in every selected run, so multiplying by ABC leaves the original signs unchanged. The simplified product identifies the confounded effect.

Sum of squares

How is the sum of squares calculated?

From contrast to sum of squares

The sum of squares of A is written SSA. For four selected runs with one measured result per run, calculate it from the A contrast.

SSA = [A]² / 4

The square removes the sign of the contrast. The result is divided by the number of runs, which is four in this example.

Using the previous results

[A] = 24 + 34 − 20 − 22 = 16
SSA = 16² / 4 = 64

We can compute SSB and SSC in the same way from [B] and [C].

However, we must check which effects are confounded before interpreting the resulting sums of squares.

Confounding

Why are the sums of squares confounded?

Equal contrasts

[A] = [BC]
[B] = [AC]
[C] = [AB]

Because each pair uses the same signed combination of results, the pair also produces the same sum of squares calculation.

Meaning of SSA, SSB and SSC

SSA = SS(A + BC)
SSB = SS(B + AC)
SSC = SS(C + AB)

The notation SS(A + BC) means that the calculation represents a confounded comparison. It does not separate A and BC into two individual sums of squares.

A large SSA cannot tell us whether the difference comes from A, BC or both.

Significance of ABC

Why can we not calculate SSABC?

ABC is +1 in all four selected runs

The runs a, b, c, abc were selected using ABC = +1. Therefore the ABC column contains +1 in every row.

ABC: +1, +1, +1, +1

This is identical to the I column, which has +1 in every run. Consequently, ABC has no separate pattern of signs in this fraction.

Why SSABC cannot be separated

Adding the four results using the ABC signs gives their total. That same total determines the overall mean.

Because the two columns are identical, we cannot distinguish an ABC interaction from the overall mean. Thus SSABC cannot be calculated or tested separately.

The same selected runs also give [A] = [BC], [B] = [AC], and [C] = [AB].

Runs per replicate

What does “per replicate” mean?

Perform each selected run once

The 23−1 design consists of the four runs a, b, c, abc for ABC = +1.

4 runs per replicate

If the entire set is performed twice, that is two replicates with eight runs in total.

Why four runs are still a fraction

Repeating the four selected combinations does not add the missing combinations (1), ab, ac, bc.

The signs of A and BC remain identical in each replicate. Therefore [A] = [BC] continues to hold.

The full 23 design requires all eight different combinations per replicate.

Method of Checking for Confounding

How to check confounding when BC = +1

Use the selected BC column

The runs (1), a, bc, abc all have BC = +1. Multiplying a sign column by BC therefore leaves it unchanged in these runs.

For example, multiplying B by BC gives

B × BC = B²C = C

Thus B and C are confounded. This can also be seen directly: B and C have matching signs in these four runs, so [B] = [C].

Check the other pairs

A × BC = ABC

A and ABC have identical signs.

B × BC = B²C = C

B and C have identical signs.

AB × BC = AB²C = AC

AB and AC have identical signs.

For BC = +1, I = BC. Since BC contains two factor letters, this selection is Resolution II. It confounds B with C, so it is unsuitable for estimating these main effects separately.

Method of Checking for Confounding

Check main effects in the 24−1 design

Begin with D = ABC

In this eight-run design, D = ABC. Multiplying both sides by D gives

D² = ABCD

Since D² = +1, every selected run satisfies ABCD = +1.

As before, multiplying any effect column by ABCD does not change its signs in the selected runs.

A × ABCD = A²BCD = BCD

Therefore A and BCD are confounded.

Repeat for B, C and D

B × ABCD = AB²CD = ACD
C × ABCD = ABC²D = ABD
D × ABCD = ABCD² = ABC

Every repeated factor disappears because its square is +1. This gives the main-effect confounding relationships:

A = BCD   B = ACD

Resolution III, IV and V

How resolution is determined

Form the defining relation from the generating columns. It contains I (the column of +1 signs) and all the products of the generating words.

The resolution is the number of factor letters in the shortest nonidentity word.

I = ABC   →   Resolution III
I = ABCD   →   Resolution IV
I = ABCDE   →   Resolution V

For more than one generator, also check their products before deciding the resolution.

How to interpret the resolution

III: A main effect can be confounded with a two-factor interaction.

IV: Main effects are not confounded with two-factor interactions, but two-factor interactions can be confounded with each other.

V: Main effects and two-factor interactions are not confounded with each other. Two-factor interactions can still be confounded with three-factor interactions.

A higher resolution generally protects more of the main effects and two-factor interactions from confounding with one another.

Summary

Summary: full and fractional 2k designs

Full and fractional designs

A full 2k experiment includes all combinations of two levels of k factors. A 2k−p fractional design includes fewer runs.

A design generator selects which sign-table rows are performed. For example, ABC = +1 selects a, b, c and abc in the 23−1 experiment.

The benefit is fewer runs. The cost is confounding.

What the calculations show

Contrasts are formed using the +1 and −1 signs. In the 23−1 fraction, [A] = [BC], [B] = [AC] and [C] = [AB].

The corresponding sums of squares represent confounded effects. The resolution of the design describes what kinds of effects may be confounded.

Resolution III, IV and V differ in how main effects and interactions are confounded.
